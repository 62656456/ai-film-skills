# Environment and scene routes

Load only the constraint packs the script-derived environment actually crosses. The listed routes are examples, not a closed taxonomy: create a custom profile or combine multiple routes when the scene contains real boundaries. Route rules constrain environmental consequences, not imagination amplitude, story function, style, palette, or future degree.

## Route A — Near-future Earth engineering

Use for terrestrial launch systems, accelerators, reactors, climate infrastructure, ocean platforms, underground laboratories, and plausible military or civil engineering.

- Start from existing geology, weather, gravity, atmosphere, labor, transport, and construction methods.
- Let atmosphere create real aerial perspective, precipitation, dust, or condensation only when weather supports them.
- Show only the access, transport/lifting, drainage, foundation, utility, service, or safety systems the selected terrestrial operation needs at the distance where they matter; roads, cranes, maintenance staging, and visible safety zones are options rather than default scenery.
- When scale matters, prove it with enough source-appropriate independent relationships—people or other bodies, ordinary vehicles, known structural units, terrain, travel/contact, displacement, or load paths—without forcing the whole list or inventing occupants.
- Derive time, weather, and motivated light from the screenplay and location. Daylight, overcast, night, interior spill, emergency lighting, and unusual physically sourced phenomena are all valid; do not require rain-night spectacle or ordinary industrial light.
- Keep the number of new physical principles small, not their visible consequences. One new machine, material, or principle may reorganize city scale, labor, infrastructure, or public life when its applicable support systems and tradeoffs remain visible; failure and aftermath appear only when the screenplay contains them.

## Route B — Airless-body surface

Use for the Moon, asteroids, and other bodies with negligible atmosphere. Specify the body and local gravity.

### Lunar south-pole baseline

- The sky remains black in daylight. The Sun stays low near the horizon and makes very long hard shadows.
- Permanently shadowed regions are not ordinary dim locations. Use vehicle lights, headlamps, instruments, weak terrain bounce, or Earth light; allow unreadable black areas.
- Lunar dust is sharp, abrasive, electrostatically adhesive, and not wind-rounded. Concentrate it on boots, seals, hinges, wheels, joints, and cleaning zones.
- Disturbed regolith follows ballistic sheets and arcs; never create a lingering sandstorm or billowing dust cloud.
- Low gravity lengthens steps and ejecta paths but preserves inertia. Heavy tools do not become weightless props.
- Power systems on ridges may track the low Sun; equipment in deep shadow needs storage, cable supply, heating, or nuclear/radioisotope power.
- Water ice should default to drilling, coring, spectroscopy, or dirty ice-bearing regolith. Do not invent a vast blue glacier unless the premise explicitly supports exposed ice.

- NASA lunar facts and gravity: https://science.nasa.gov/moon/facts/

## Route C — Orbital and deep-space exterior

Use for rendezvous, docking, EVA, repair, orbital industry, telescopes, and free-flying vehicles.

- Define the target-relative frame. Both craft can move extremely fast in orbit while final closure remains slow.
- For rendezvous or docking, align the applicable docking axis, attitude, sensors, capture system, and collision-avoidance space. For a non-docking exterior, use the subject-relative frame and do not invent a capture ring. Do not stage a high-speed nose-first charge unless the source explicitly establishes an impact event and its physics.
- Use short, local attitude-control pulses only when required. Avoid persistent fiery exhaust and plumes directed at arrays, radiators, docking faces, or crew.
- When EVA or free-body manipulation is present, make reaction paths visible through the applicable handrails, tethers, restraints, robotic/manipulator arms, tool retention, jets, counter-rotation, or another supported method; an uncrewed rendezvous need not acquire EVA hardware.
- In direct sun, maintain one hard shadow direction and extreme dynamic range. A properly exposed sunlit craft may show few or no stars.
- Use only the thermal, optical, power, communication, access, protection, impact, and repair features the craft and current state require; MLI, radiators, arrays, antennas, handrails, pits, and patches are examples rather than a package.
- In LEO, localized atomic-oxygen erosion and micrometeoroid/orbital-debris damage are plausible; do not use rain rust or mud.

- NASA EVA tethers and handrails: https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/for-kids-and-students/what-is-a-spacewalk-grades-5-8/

## Route D — Dense alien atmosphere and hydrocarbon sea

Use the Titan baseline only when Titan or a closely justified analog is explicit.

### Titan baseline

- Titan has a dense nitrogen-rich atmosphere, orange organic haze, weak sunlight, methane weather, low gravity, and a cryogenic surface.
- Surface daylight is dim, diffuse, low-contrast, and orange-brown. Do not show black sky, crisp stars, blue daylight, or an enormous razor-sharp Saturn.
- Water ice behaves like rock; seas and lakes contain liquid hydrocarbons. Avoid liquid-water waterfalls, green plants, and warm Earth coastlines.
- Use a dark, often smooth or lightly rippled sea by default. Large whitecapped waves require explicit strong-weather evidence.
- Human equipment needs oxygen supply, strong insulation, heating, seals, and cryogenic-compatible materials. External pressure is not vacuum, so the suit need not look like a highly inflated EVA suit.
- Weak sunlight favors radioisotope/nuclear power and careful waste-heat use rather than large ordinary solar farms.
- A floating platform needs sufficient displacement in a lower-density liquid and low gravity. Do not copy a heavy steel Earth submarine unchanged.
- Prefer electric pump-jet, propeller, rotor, or other medium-appropriate propulsion. Open combustion requires carried oxidizer.

- NASA Titan facts: https://science.nasa.gov/saturn/moons/titan/facts/
- NASA Dragonfly science and atmosphere: https://science.nasa.gov/mission/dragonfly/science/
- NASA Dragonfly power and spacecraft: https://science.nasa.gov/mission/dragonfly/spacecraft-and-instruments/
- NASA Titan submarine concept: https://www.nasa.gov/general/titan-submarine-exploring-the-depths-of-kraken/

## Route E — Crewed interior

Use for control rooms, airlocks, laboratories, cabins, service tunnels, pressure locks, and vehicle interiors.

- State gravity source and down direction. A transit cabin may be microgravity, thrust gravity, or rotation-derived; do not assume Earth gravity.
- Organize surfaces around body restraint, reach, visibility, equipment removal, cable routing, pressure zoning, ventilation, and emergency isolation.
- When a visible interface exists, derive its display, cue, carrier, angle, reach, sensing, governance, and feedback from the actual body or autonomous task; keep decorative UI secondary. A human display or hand action is not mandatory.
- Choose only the source- and route-applicable visible practical, window, instrument, task, environmental, emissive-material, or declared nonvisual signal sources. Do not force several source types into one interior; avoid a blue fog volume with no source.
- Show the volume, contact or clearance logic, stowage, service strategy, labels, condition, and body interaction that the source and operating history actually require. Do not add wear, repair access, labels, or human hands as generic realism props.
- When the screenplay contains a failure event, choose a physically coherent consequence such as pressure loss, thermal fault, power isolation, navigation error, bearing vibration, fluid leak, or impact alarm. Do not add failure to a quiet, relational, routine, or reference scene.

## Route F — Rotating habitat or orbital industry

Use for rings, tethers, centrifuges, mining plants, shipyards, refineries, and large artificial-gravity structures.

- For a rotating habitat/centrifuge, state ring radius, rotation rate, and target gravity when numbers matter; for qualitative images, state a plausible relation. A tether, non-rotating shipyard, refinery, or mine uses its own reference-frame fields instead.
- In a rotating habitat, artificial down points away from the axis; place inhabited surfaces according to its chosen topology. A hub, tether, shipyard, refinery, or free-flying industry is not required to have a rim floor.
- In a non-rotating hub, remove the visual grammar of a gravity floor. When occupants or loose/retained items are present, use multiple roll angles, multi-surface restraints, taut or floating lines, clothing, tools, fluid, dust, organisms, or cargo that obey microgravity; an empty hub may prove the frame through architecture and free-body trajectories instead of workers. Foot loops alone do not prove microgravity.
- Radial movement and fast head/body motion can reveal Coriolis effects. Use handrails, controlled movement, and curved trajectories where readable.
- Where rotating and non-rotating zones meet, reveal an applicable transfer strategy such as bearings, locks, seals, contactless transfer, articulated transit, or another explicit mechanism. Do not add that boundary to a wholly rotating or wholly free-flying system.
- Trace the actual load path for the chosen subtype: rim/spokes/hub only for a ring, tensile members/end masses for a tether, keel/truss/foundation for a yard or refinery, or another explicit topology. Reject decoration with no support logic, not the absence of ring parts.
- High-power industry requires source-appropriate heat rejection, working-fluid or solid-state transport where used, shielding where the hazard requires it, material handling, and a credible maintenance strategy. Maintenance may be human, remote, robotic, autonomous, biological, self-healing, replace-by-module, or shutdown-based according to the screenplay and mechanism.
- Keep plumes away from arrays, radiators, raw material, crew, and docking corridors.
- Exterior maintenance on a fast rotating rim is hazardous; use a source-derived strategy such as shutdown, non-rotating service access, remote or autonomous systems, replaceable modules, self-maintaining materials, or explicitly protected human/nonhuman work that accounts for tangential speed.

## Route-selection audit

- Has the script-derived environment profile and every crossed boundary been named?
- Are atmosphere, gravity, light, temperature, power, heat, motion, and wear specific to that environment?
- Are cross-route effects included only where a real boundary exists?
- Has the prompt avoided importing familiar Earth weather, game-space fog, terrestrial rust, or aircraft behavior into an incompatible route?
